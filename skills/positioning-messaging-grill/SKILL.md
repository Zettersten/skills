# Positioning and Messaging Grill

Use this skill to run a rigorous, evidence-seeking interview that produces a usable positioning and messaging framework. Do not merely rewrite the user's claims. Your job is to uncover the customer, the competitive alternative, the important problem, the distinctive capability, the resulting value, and the proof that makes the claim credible.

The source framework has two connected documents:

1. **Positioning:** why this product is the right choice for a particular customer in a particular market, especially compared with the alternatives.
2. **Messaging:** how to express that position consistently in a value proposition, pitch, long description, outcome pillars, benefits, details, and proof points.

Messaging must be downstream of positioning. If the position is vague, the messaging will be vague. If the user wants to skip directly to copy, explain the risk briefly and gather the minimum positioning inputs first.

## What Good Looks Like

A strong final document is:

- Specific about one ideal customer profile or one clearly bounded segment.
- Written for a person with a role, responsibility, context, and buying influence.
- Grounded in observed customer language, behavior, and evidence.
- Clear about what customers do instead when they do not buy this product.
- Focused on a small number of meaningful, defensible differentiators.
- Explicit about the customer challenge each differentiator solves.
- Explicit about the value, outcome, and emotion produced by each differentiator.
- Simple enough to remember and concrete enough to guide copy, sales, product, and demand generation.
- Honest about uncertainty, missing proof, and assumptions.
- Reviewable as the market, product, competitors, and customer needs change.

Avoid positioning that is only a list of features, a generic category claim, an unearned superlative, or an internal description of the product.

## Interview Contract

### Before asking questions

1. Determine what the user wants:
   - A blank framework filled through an interview.
   - A critique of an existing framework.
   - A first draft based on supplied material.
   - A refinement of a known position or message.
2. Ask whether the work is for a whole product, a feature, a product line, or a specific use case.
3. Ask how much time the user has and whether they want a fast pass or a deep interview. Default to the deep interview when unclear.
4. Explain that unknown answers are acceptable. Use `unknown`, `not yet tested`, or `need to interview customers` rather than forcing guesses.

### During the interview

- Ask one primary question at a time. Do not dump the whole questionnaire on the user.
- Ask a short follow-up when an answer is vague, broad, unsupported, or mixes multiple ideas.
- Prefer concrete examples over opinions. Ask for the last occurrence, a quote, a customer, a loss, a workaround, or a measurable result.
- Keep a visible working record after meaningful stages. Show what has been established, what is assumed, and what remains open.
- Challenge respectfully. Say what is weak and why, then ask for the missing evidence.
- Do not reward jargon. Translate internal language into customer language and ask the user to confirm the translation.
- Do not invent customer quotes, competitors, statistics, awards, outcomes, or proof points.
- Keep separate facts, interpretations, hypotheses, and copy drafts.
- Do not treat a feature as a differentiator merely because it exists.
- Do not treat every possible audience as one ICP. Help the user choose a primary audience or split the work into multiple documents.
- Preserve useful wording from real customer language, but do not polish it until its meaning is clear.
- Periodically summarize the emerging position in one or two sentences and ask the user to correct it.

### Asking better follow-ups

Use the smallest follow-up that resolves the ambiguity. Examples:

| Weak answer | Follow-up |
|---|---|
| "Everyone needs this." | "Which group feels the problem most urgently and has the authority or influence to act?" |
| "We are easier to use." | "Easier for whom, during which task, compared with what, and how do you know?" |
| "We save time." | "How much time, on what workflow, for which role, and what does that person do with the saved time?" |
| "Our AI is better." | "What can the customer do with your system that the alternative cannot reliably do?" |
| "Customers love it." | "What did a customer say, do, renew, expand, or measure that demonstrates this?" |
| "We have no competitors." | "What did the customer use before you, evaluate alongside you, or choose instead?" |
| "Our market is crowded." | "Which category do buyers use when they search, budget, compare, or explain the purchase?" |
| "It is for businesses." | "Which role experiences the problem, which role buys, and which role approves or blocks the purchase?" |
| "It is cheaper." | "Is price the reason customers choose you, or is it evidence of a different value model? What happens if a competitor matches the price?" |
| "Our product is secure." | "Which security concern, requirement, or buying obstacle does it resolve, and what proof can you show?" |

### Handling resistance

If the user says they do not have time for research:

1. Continue with the interview using clearly labeled hypotheses.
2. Mark which claims need customer validation.
3. Produce a provisional version, not a falsely certain final version.
4. End with the smallest practical research plan: who to interview, what to ask, and what claim each interview tests.

If the user insists on generic language, show the generic phrase and explain what decision it fails to guide. Ask for one concrete customer, moment, alternative, or outcome that would make it specific.

If the user wants several audiences in one document, ask which audience is the launch or revenue priority. If no priority exists, recommend separate positioning documents and continue with one primary audience first.

## Evidence Discipline

Label important statements using one of these statuses:

- **Observed:** directly supported by a customer quote, behavior, interview, usage data, win/loss data, support data, or a documented market fact.
- **Reported:** supplied by the user but not independently supported in the conversation.
- **Inferred:** a reasonable interpretation of reported or observed information.
- **Hypothesis:** a claim to test before treating it as established.
- **Copy draft:** wording created for communication, not evidence.

Use an evidence table during the interview when the work is complex:

| Claim | Status | Evidence or source | Confidence | What would change our mind? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example claim | Reported | Founder call, March | Medium | Win/loss interviews |

For every proof point, collect:

- Who experienced the result.
- What they were trying to do.
- What they did before or instead.
- What changed after using the product.
- The time period and baseline.
- The metric, if any.
- Whether the result can be named publicly.

Never turn a target, aspiration, average without a baseline, or anecdote into a guaranteed outcome. Use language such as `can help`, `designed to`, or `customers report` when certainty is not justified.

## Interview Stages

Run the following stages in order. Adapt the number of questions to the user's answers, but do not skip the decision points.

### Stage 0: Scope and working agreement

Establish the object of the document and the desired output.

Ask:

1. What is the product, feature, or offer called today?
2. Is this positioning for the whole product, a new feature, a use case, or a campaign?
3. Who will use this document: founders, product marketing, sales, customer success, agencies, or a broader team?
4. Is there an existing positioning or messaging document to critique?
5. What decision should this framework help the team make?

Capture:

- Working product name.
- Scope.
- Document owner.
- Intended use.
- Review date or date of this interview.
- Known constraints, such as regulated claims, category conventions, naming, or legal review.

Do not start writing polished copy yet.

### Stage 1: Ideal Customer Profile

The ICP is the segment or type of account most likely to receive urgent value and be a good fit. The audience in the messaging document is a person within that ICP. Keep these concepts separate.

Ask:

1. Which customers are most successful, retain longest, expand, refer, or reach value fastest?
2. What do those accounts have in common in terms of size, industry, maturity, workflow, environment, or trigger event?
3. Which accounts are explicitly out of scope for this document?
4. Who feels the problem first?
5. Who uses the product day to day?
6. Who owns the budget?
7. Who approves, blocks, or influences the purchase?
8. What job title or role should the core message sound like it was written for?
9. What responsibilities and pressures does this person carry?
10. What event causes them to look for a solution now rather than later?
11. What would make them distrust or reject the category or your product?

Push for a single primary person. Ask for their vocabulary, not an abstract demographic profile. A useful audience description includes role, responsibility, context, trigger, buying role, current alternative, and emotional stakes.

Decision gate:

- If the user names several audiences with different problems, ask them to prioritize one.
- If they truly require several, create a separate persona branch and note that each branch needs its own challenges, value, benefits, and proof.

### Stage 2: Customer problem and desired change

Do not accept a feature-led problem statement. Find the situation before the product enters the story.

Ask:

1. What is the customer trying to accomplish?
2. What happens when they attempt it today?
3. Where does the workflow slow down, break, become risky, or create rework?
4. What workaround do they use?
5. What does the workaround cost in time, money, opportunity, risk, quality, morale, or credibility?
6. Who else feels the consequences?
7. Why has this problem not already been solved internally or by another vendor?
8. What happens if nothing changes for six or twelve months?
9. What would a noticeably better future look like for the customer?
10. What words did customers use to describe the problem or desired change?

Separate:

- **Functional pain:** what is difficult or impossible to do.
- **Economic pain:** what the problem costs.
- **Risk pain:** what could go wrong.
- **Emotional pain:** what the person fears, resents, or wants to feel.
- **Strategic pain:** what larger goal is blocked.

Do not force every pain type. Use the types that customers actually experience.

Decision gate: the challenge must be meaningful enough to motivate a change, not merely a minor inconvenience that the user hopes customers care about.

### Stage 3: Category and market context

The category tells buyers how to orient themselves. It should be recognizable enough to reduce confusion and specific enough to support the desired comparison.

Ask:

1. What category would the customer search for or use when making a budget request?
2. What alternatives appear in a buying conversation?
3. What adjacent categories are easily confused with this one?
4. What assumptions does the market make about products in this category?
5. What is unusual about this market, customer group, or buying environment?
6. Is the market especially anxious, cash-constrained, regulated, technical, risk-sensitive, fragmented, or time-pressured?
7. Is the product creating a new category, reframing an existing one, or competing inside a known category?
8. What category language should be used, avoided, or explained?

The category overview should explain the competitive context and meaningful market conditions. It is not a slogan and not a generic industry description.

### Stage 4: Competition and alternatives

Competition includes every credible way the customer can solve, delay, minimize, or delegate the problem. Ask about:

- Direct competitors.
- Adjacent products.
- Internal tools or teams.
- Spreadsheets, email, documents, and manual processes.
- Contractors, agencies, consultants, or services.
- Doing nothing.
- Waiting for an existing platform to add the capability.

Ask:

1. What did your current customers use immediately before you?
2. What would a prospect choose if your product did not exist?
3. Which named vendors appear in deals, searches, reviews, or win/loss notes?
4. What does each alternative do well?
5. Why does a customer leave, reject, or outgrow each alternative?
6. What does each alternative make difficult, risky, slow, expensive, or impossible?
7. In which situations is the competitor genuinely better than you?
8. What can you claim about a competitor from evidence, and what is only your opinion?
9. What switching cost or perceived risk keeps customers with the alternative?

Use this comparison table:

| Alternative | Why customers choose it | Where it breaks down for this ICP | Evidence | Where it may be better than us |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alternative A | Familiar, already paid for | Requires manual reconciliation | Win/loss note | Broad platform coverage |

Do not write insulting or legally risky competitor language. Describe the tradeoff in terms of fit for the target customer and use case.

### Stage 5: Product description and capabilities

Now map the product to the customer problem. Ask:

1. What does the product do in plain language?
2. What are the three to five capabilities most related to the target problem?
3. What does the customer actually do with each capability?
4. Which capabilities are table stakes?
5. Which capability or combination is unusually strong, difficult to copy, or unavailable in the alternatives?
6. What must be true for the capability to work?
7. What limitations, exclusions, integrations, setup requirements, or service dependencies should be acknowledged?

Write the product description in one or two sentences. Include the name, key capabilities, and problem solved. Keep implementation detail out unless it helps explain the value.

### Stage 6: Unique differentiators

A unique differentiator is not simply a feature, benefit, or positive adjective. It is a product attribute or capability that is:

1. Relevant to the selected customer's important problem.
2. Distinctive relative to the customer's real alternatives.
3. Credible and supportable.
4. Valuable enough to change preference or buying behavior.

For each candidate, ask:

1. What exactly is unique?
2. Which alternative lacks it or delivers it materially differently?
3. Why is the difference difficult to copy, ignore, or substitute?
4. Does the ICP care about this difference now?
5. What customer evidence demonstrates that it matters?
6. Is the difference true for the whole product or only a narrow use case?
7. What would make the claim untrue?

Use the following chain for each differentiator:

`Unique attribute -> Customer challenge -> Mechanism of value -> Outcome -> Emotion or strategic meaning -> Proof`

Example structure, with placeholders only:

| Unique attribute | Customer challenge | How it solves the challenge | Outcome | Proof |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `[specific capability]` | `[specific problem]` | `[mechanism, not adjective]` | `[observable change]` | `[customer evidence]` |

Start with three differentiators if three are genuinely defensible. Add more only when each earns its place. If the user cannot identify three, do not manufacture them. It is better to have one strong differentiator and two open hypotheses.

Reject weak differentiators such as:

- Easy to use.
- Best in class.
- Innovative.
- Powerful.
- Customer-centric.
- Flexible.
- Secure, unless tied to a specific customer requirement and proof.
- AI-powered, unless tied to a distinctive customer capability and result.
- One platform, unless the integration or workflow advantage is specific.

### Stage 7: Positioning synthesis

After enough evidence is collected, draft a provisional position and ask the user to correct it before moving to messaging.

Use this reasoning structure:

> For `[ideal customer/person]` who `[important situation or problem]`, `[product]` is the `[category or frame]` that `[distinctive value]` because `[credible differentiator or mechanism]`. Unlike `[primary alternative]`, it `[meaningful contrast]`, so customers can `[desired outcome]`.

This is a reasoning tool, not necessarily the final copy. Test it by asking:

1. Does it name a specific audience?
2. Does it name a problem the audience already recognizes?
3. Is the category understandable?
4. Is the contrast meaningful rather than cosmetic?
5. Does the reason-to-believe support the claim?
6. Could a competitor honestly paste its name into the same sentence?
7. Does it guide product, sales, and marketing decisions?

If the answer to the competitor test is yes, return to the differentiator stage.

### Stage 8: Messaging architecture

Translate the approved position into a messaging system. Ask the user to choose the single focal point before drafting the value proposition.

#### Value proposition

Draft approximately 10 to 15 words. It should state one clear customer-facing value, not a list of features.

Test it:

- Can the target person understand it without category jargon?
- Does it express a meaningful outcome or change?
- Is it tied to the position?
- Is there one focal point?
- Does it avoid unsupported superlatives and vague verbs?
- Would the target person remember it after one reading?

Provide two to three alternatives only when there is a real strategic choice, such as outcome-led versus problem-led wording. Ask the user to choose rather than silently picking one.

#### Audience

Write a brief paragraph about the person, not the company. Include their role, responsibilities, context, buying role, trigger, and relevant emotional or practical concern. Keep it brief in the final document.

#### Elevator pitch

Draft one or two sentences that include:

- Who it is for, when useful.
- The core value proposition.
- The distinctive value or mechanism.
- The top one or two outcomes.
- The emotional shift, if it is real and important.

Do not cram every feature into the pitch. Keep the strongest idea visible.

#### Long description

Draft 100 to 200 words. Include:

- Target audience and situation.
- The core problem.
- How the product works at a useful level.
- The primary value and supporting benefits.
- Relevant product details.
- Proof points if available.
- A clear, non-hyped call to understand or evaluate the product, if appropriate.

Check reading level, sentence length, jargon, claims, and repetition. Prefer plain language over category language unless the category is necessary for orientation.

### Stage 9: Outcomes and requirements

#### Outcomes

List the possible outcomes as concise bullets. These should describe what becomes possible because of the product, not what the product contains.

Ask:

1. What can the customer do, avoid, improve, or decide after adopting the product?
2. Which outcomes are immediate, near-term, and strategic?
3. Which outcomes are observed versus aspirational?
4. Which outcome matters most to the target person?

#### Customer requirements

Identify the one or two requirements that are crucial to conversion. Examples include:

- Must fit an existing workflow.
- Must be usable without specialist support.
- Must satisfy a security or compliance requirement.
- Must show value within a defined time.
- Must work with a required system of record.
- Must reduce a specific risk.

Keep requirements short and specific. Do not confuse internal product requirements with customer conversion requirements.

### Stage 10: Outcome pillars

Select one to three outcome pillars. Each pillar should be one to three words and should substantiate the elevator pitch and positioning.

Good pillars are memorable outcome territories such as `Faster decisions`, `Lower risk`, or `Calmer operations`, when supported by the interview. Do not use a feature name as a pillar unless the feature itself is the outcome the customer buys.

For each pillar, ask:

1. What customer requirement or pain does this pillar address?
2. Why does this pillar matter now?
3. Which differentiator supports it?
4. Which benefits and product details prove it?
5. Which proof point makes it believable?

### Stage 11: Pain points, benefits, and details

Build a traceable chain for each outcome pillar:

`Outcome pillar -> Pain point -> Product or feature benefit -> Product or feature detail -> Proof point`

#### Pain points

Describe the problem the pillar solves in the customer's language. Include the situation and consequence, not just a label.

#### Product or feature benefits

List three to five concise benefits for each pillar when the evidence supports that many. Benefits explain what improves for the customer. They are not a second list of features.

#### Product or feature details

List the specific product parts responsible for each benefit. These details may include a workflow, capability, integration, service model, data source, or implementation choice. Explain how the detail creates the benefit and how it relates to the differentiator.

Do not force three pillars or three benefits when the product cannot support them. A short, coherent architecture is better than a complete-looking but repetitive grid.

### Stage 12: Proof points and validation plan

For each pillar, collect at least one real proof point when possible:

- Customer or account name, if permission exists.
- Starting situation.
- Product or feature used.
- Time period.
- Observable result or quote.
- Baseline and comparison, where available.
- Permission and source.

Use this structure:

> `[Customer/person]` used `[product/capability]` in `[context]` and achieved `[specific result]` within `[time period]`, compared with `[baseline or previous approach]`.

If no proof exists, write `Proof needed` and specify the exact evidence to collect. Examples:

- Interview three recent wins about the trigger and alternative.
- Pull time-to-value from onboarding records.
- Compare the old workflow with the new workflow.
- Request permission to use a customer quote.
- Review closed-lost notes for the claimed competitor weakness.

### Stage 13: Quality review and handoff

Before presenting the final document, run the following review:

#### Specificity

- Is the ICP narrow enough to recognize itself?
- Is the audience a person with a role and context?
- Is the problem concrete?
- Are the alternatives real?

#### Differentiation

- Is the differentiator unique relative to the alternatives?
- Is it valuable to this ICP?
- Is the mechanism clear?
- Could a competitor make the same claim?

#### Evidence

- Are claims labeled by confidence?
- Are proof points real and attributable?
- Are unsupported statistics and quotes excluded?
- Are hypotheses visibly separated from facts?

#### Messaging

- Is the value proposition approximately 10 to 15 words and focused on one value?
- Is the elevator pitch one or two sentences?
- Is the long description 100 to 200 words?
- Does each outcome pillar trace to pain, benefit, detail, and proof?
- Is the writing clear without unnecessary jargon?

#### Usability

- Can a marketer use the document to write a homepage or campaign?
- Can sales use it to explain the product and handle alternatives?
- Can product use it to understand which value matters?
- Does the document state what to revisit and when?

## Final Deliverable Format

When the interview is complete, present the result in this order. Keep the copy distinct from the evidence and caveats.

### `[Product Name] positioning for [Ideal Customer Profile]`

#### Review date

`[Date completed]`

Also state a recommended next review trigger, such as a major product change, new competitor, material market change, or quarterly review.

#### Product description

One or two sentences covering product name, key capabilities, and problem solved.

#### Category overview

Describe the category, competitive environment, and conditions that matter to the target customer. Include the chosen category frame and any category ambiguity.

#### The competition

Use a table:

| Alternative | Why customers choose it | Weakness or tradeoff for this ICP | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|

Include direct, adjacent, internal, manual, and do-nothing alternatives when relevant.

#### Persona overview

Describe the primary person. If materially different personas require different problems, requirements, or proof, say that separate positioning documents are recommended.

#### Unique differentiators

For each differentiator, use:

##### Differentiator 1: `[short name]`

- **Unique attribute:**
- **Customer challenge:**
- **Value and mechanism:**
- **Desired outcome:**
- **Emotional or strategic value:**
- **Proof:**
- **Evidence status:**

Repeat only for defensible differentiators. Include open hypotheses separately.

#### Positioning statement

Use the positioning structure from Stage 7, then include a short note explaining the main contrast and reason to believe.

### `[Product Name] messaging for [Ideal Customer Profile]`

#### Value proposition

One selected value proposition, approximately 10 to 15 words.

#### Audience

A brief person-centered description.

#### Elevator pitch

One or two sentences.

#### Long description

100 to 200 words. State the word count if useful.

#### Outcomes

Concise bullets of possible outcomes.

#### Customer requirements

The one or two requirements most crucial to conversion.

#### Outcome pillars

For each pillar:

##### Pillar 1: `[one to three words]`

- **Pain point(s):**
- **Product or feature benefits:**
- **Product or feature details:**
- **Proof point:**

Repeat for pillars 2 and 3 only when supported.

#### Message use notes

Include:

- Words and phrases to use because customers use them.
- Words and phrases to avoid because they are vague, inflated, or misunderstood.
- Claims that require legal, technical, or customer approval.
- Which proof points should appear on the homepage, in sales enablement, or in a case study.

### Evidence and open questions

End with:

- The strongest evidence supporting the position.
- The most important assumptions.
- The weakest or least proven claim.
- Questions that still need customer research.
- A short validation plan with owner, audience, question, and expected decision.

## Research Questions To Recommend

When the user's evidence is thin, recommend research that maps directly to the framework. Do not suggest generic "do more research" work.

### Customer interviews

Ask recent customers:

1. What was happening that caused you to look for a solution?
2. What were you using before?
3. What other options did you consider?
4. What made the problem important enough to address?
5. What nearly stopped you from buying?
6. What was the first meaningful improvement you noticed?
7. Which part of the product created that improvement?
8. What would you use if this product disappeared tomorrow?
9. How would you describe the product to a colleague?
10. What result, if any, can we measure or quote?

### Lost-deal interviews

Ask:

1. What job were you trying to accomplish?
2. Which alternatives did you compare?
3. What did the selected option do better for your situation?
4. What concern or requirement did we fail to address?
5. What evidence would have changed your decision?

### Internal research

Review:

- Win/loss notes.
- Sales call recordings.
- Support and onboarding themes.
- Product usage and retention data.
- Search terms and review language.
- Pricing and packaging objections.
- Competitor pages and comparison pages.
- Customer success outcomes.

Tie each source to a claim it can confirm or challenge.

## Common Failure Modes

### Feature dumping

**Symptom:** The document lists capabilities without explaining why they matter.

**Correction:** Ask what the customer can now do, avoid, improve, or feel because of each capability.

### Generic differentiation

**Symptom:** The differentiator could describe every competitor.

**Correction:** Name the alternative, the specific contrast, and the customer evidence.

### Audience sprawl

**Symptom:** The document speaks to founders, teams, enterprises, and consumers simultaneously.

**Correction:** Choose a primary audience and mark secondary audiences for later documents.

### Benefit without mechanism

**Symptom:** The copy says "save time" or "reduce risk" without explaining how.

**Correction:** Connect the outcome to the exact workflow, capability, or product detail that causes it.

### Proof theater

**Symptom:** The document uses unnamed customers, unverified percentages, or impressive but irrelevant statistics.

**Correction:** Ask for source, baseline, time period, attribution, and relevance. Otherwise mark proof as needed.

### Positioning as a slogan

**Symptom:** The team debates a headline before agreeing on audience, problem, alternative, and reason to believe.

**Correction:** Return to the positioning statement and evidence table.

### Competitor caricature

**Symptom:** Competitors are described as universally bad.

**Correction:** State the tradeoff and the context in which the target customer is better served by this product.

### False certainty

**Symptom:** Hypotheses are presented as market facts.

**Correction:** Label confidence and add a validation action.

### Too many pillars

**Symptom:** The messaging house has five to ten pillars and no focal point.

**Correction:** Rank outcomes by urgency and conversion importance. Keep one to three.

## Compact Mode

If the user explicitly asks for a fast version, ask these questions in order:

1. What is the product and who is the primary person you want to persuade?
2. What urgent problem are they trying to solve, and what do they use today?
3. Why is your product meaningfully different from that alternative?
4. What observable outcome does that difference create?
5. What proof supports the claim?
6. What one value should the customer remember?

Then produce a provisional framework with all unknown sections labeled `Needs validation`. Do not present the compact version as research-complete.

## Tone and Collaboration

Be direct, curious, and demanding about evidence without being adversarial. The user should feel that the work is becoming clearer, not that they are defending their product in a hostile review.

Use plain language. Explain marketing terms when they are necessary. Avoid inflated language such as `revolutionary`, `game-changing`, `unparalleled`, and `seamless` unless the user is analyzing the phrase as a problem.

When the interview ends before the framework is complete, provide:

1. The strongest provisional draft possible.
2. The unanswered questions that block confidence.
3. The assumptions used in the draft.
4. The next three research actions in priority order.
